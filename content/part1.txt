# 1 | The wrong classroom
@1
So after a long time, we finally met. My one and only one, apdi nu dhaan solluven. Small correction: at the beginning of this story, I hadn't met him yet. 😔

I was Shreya, sixteen, Class Eleven, professional overthinker, occasional maths survivor. My idea of romance involved rain, a good soundtrack, and somebody understanding why the last piece of chocolate was emotionally important.

Real life gave me a leaking classroom and a teacher holding forty-seven wet library cards.

“Shreya,” Revathi ma'am said, “I need a favour.”

That was how the trouble started.
@2
The favour was supposed to take ten minutes. Carry the attendance register to the old reading room. Leave it on the desk. Return before chemistry.

Except the reading-room door was stuck, my shoelace was open, and a boy on the other side pulled exactly when I pushed.

I landed against a stack of dictionaries. He caught the top three before they hit my head.

“Usually people knock,” he said.

“Usually doors open. Idhu door-ah, entrance exam-ah?”

Ma'am appeared behind him. “Excellent. You've met your partner.”

Partner? For what? My funeral after chemistry attendance?
@3
He belonged to Class Ten. Fifteen years old. Exactly one year younger than me, as I would discover through an extremely unnecessary argument about birthday candles.

His name was on the register. I read it that afternoon. Let me be honest about that now: he wasn't a secret agent, and our school wasn't allergic to introductions.

But in these pages, I'm keeping that name folded away for a while.

First, you should meet the boy I met: ink on his thumb, one crooked shoelace, and the patience to rescue a dictionary before making fun of me.
@4
The monsoon had damaged the little neighbourhood reading centre our school supported. Revathi ma'am wanted two students to catalogue donated books and prepare a reading programme for younger children.

“Shreya writes well. You organise well,” she told him. “Work here after lunch, with the library staff. Tell your parents about Saturday sessions.”

I heard extra work. He heard books.

Enakku homework-ey podhum; ippo social service vera. I sighed as if ma'am had personally cancelled my retirement.

“Can we save the ones with torn covers?” he asked.

Ma'am smiled. “We can try.”

He picked up the ugliest book first.

For reasons I didn't examine, that annoyed me less than it should have.
@5
Our first disagreement concerned labels. I wanted blue for stories and yellow for reference. He wanted alphabetical order.

“Children don't enter a library thinking, today I feel like the letter P.”

“They can still find things alphabetically.”

“They can find joy in blue. Konjam imagination use pannu, please.”

He considered this with ridiculous seriousness. Then he divided the blue labels alphabetically.

Compromise looked suspiciously like neat handwriting.

When the bell rang, I discovered he'd put aside a dry notebook for me. My chemistry notes had been under the register during the rain.

“Blue,” he said. “For your tragic stories.”
@6
Nila, my best friend, met me outside chemistry with the expression of a television reporter at a crime scene.

“Why are you smiling?”

“I'm not.”

“Un face-ku andha information sollu first. It's smiling without permission.”

I explained the library, the door, the boy. She became interested at exactly the wrong word.

“One year younger, Shreya.”

“Yes. Not one century.”

Our teacher asked why we were whispering. Nila said we were discussing bonding.

Technically, chemistry. Morally, betrayal.

That evening I covered the dry notebook in brown paper. I wrote my name neatly, then wondered whether he'd notice.
@7
He noticed the crooked label instead.

“That's going to peel off.”

“Unakku vera velaiye illaya? Good afternoon to you too.”

He offered the glue. I refused out of dignity, then borrowed it when the label immediately peeled off.

We worked at opposite ends of a table while the librarian, Mrs. Mary, repaired bindings. Somewhere between adventure stories and atlases, he asked what I liked reading.

“Books where the girl has a personality outside the boy.”

“Good standard.”

“And where the boy is less irritating than you.”

He slid me a mystery novel. “Try this. The boy disappears in chapter two.”
@8
Saturday arrived with parental permission, two lunch boxes, and Nila volunteering because she enjoyed free entertainment.

At the reading centre, a little girl called Thara refused every book except one missing its ending.

“Someone tore out the last page,” she said, furious.

He sat on the floor a respectful distance away. “Shall we make another ending until we find it?”

Thara demanded a dragon, a bus conductor, and an apology.

He included all three.

I laughed so loudly the centre coordinator looked up.

He looked up too. Just once. Then he lost his place in the story.
@9
On the bus home, Nila took the window seat and promptly pretended to sleep. I stood beside the pole; he stood near the door with the donated books.

At a sharp turn, my pen rolled under a seat. He retrieved it without dropping the carton.

“Do you practise being useful?” I asked.

“Straight-ah thanks solla maatiya? Or is teasing your official language?”

“Romba thanks, your highness.”

That earned a smile I could still draw from memory, despite being terrible at drawing.

When I got down, he lifted the carton in farewell.

I nearly waved at the books.
@10
That night I opened the blue notebook to do chemistry. A scrap of paper fell out.

Not a love letter. Calm down. It was an inventory list.

At the bottom, below fourteen damaged picture books, someone had written: Find an ending for Thara.

I wrote beneath it: The dragon should apologise first.

Then I stared at my reply. Was that weird? Was replying to stationery an advanced symptom? Aiyo, Shreya. Oru paper-kitta poi ivlo scene-ah?

I put the scrap back before I could change my mind.

On Monday, the old reading room was locked.

A notice on the door said the project had been suspended.
# 2 | Things written in blue
@11
“Suspended means postponed,” Nila said, reading over my shoulder. “It doesn't mean your boyfriend has been expelled.”

“He isn't my anything.”

“Seri, seri. Un alphabet consultant, then. Happy-ah?”

The school needed a safety inspection after another ceiling leak. Revathi ma'am moved the books to an unused staff workroom and assigned different lunch slots so students wouldn't crowd it.

I got Mondays and Wednesdays. He got Tuesdays and Thursdays.

An excellent system for the school.

A deeply irritating system for someone who had spent Sunday deciding how casually to return an inventory list.
@12
Mrs. Mary became our unwilling postal service.

“Leave project notes in the blue folder,” she said. “Actual project notes. I have lived through enough adolescent handwriting.”

I left instructions about the labels and, at the bottom, the dragon's apology.

On Wednesday, his reply waited under a paper clip.

The dragon says sorry, but the bus conductor wants exact change.

I pressed my lips together so hard my face hurt.

Underneath, he had done every label exactly as we'd agreed.

Being listened to, I discovered, could be terribly inconvenient for a person's peace of mind.
@13
Our notes became a small country with its own laws. Book counts first. Jokes below the line. No names in the jokes, because Mrs. Mary said she didn't need witnesses for her headache.

He drew a tiny umbrella beside books that needed drying.

I began calling him Umbrella Boy in my diary.

Very original, Shreya. Enna oru creativity. Literature departments everywhere were trembling.

At home, Amma asked whether the library project was going well.

“Fine.”

“Then why are you buttering an empty plate?”

I looked down. My toast was still in the toaster.
@14
On Friday, both classes were called to the hall for a reading demonstration. He stood with his classmates near the stage.

I raised the blue folder. He touched two fingers to his forehead in a tiny salute.

Then a girl beside him adjusted his collar, and the entire imaginary orchestra in my head stopped playing.

She wore our school uniform and looked perfectly comfortable beside him.

I spent the demonstration writing exceptionally aggressive notes about punctuation.

When Nila asked what was wrong, I said nothing.

Nothing had somehow acquired a face, a braid, and excellent collar-adjusting privileges.
@15
I could have asked him. Instead, I did what every sensible sixteen-year-old does: invented a complicated tragedy while arranging dictionaries.

My next project note contained no joke.

His reply contained a question mark beside the empty space.

On Wednesday he waited outside the staff workroom, in full view of Mrs. Mary.

“Did I miss a task?”

“No.”

“Did I upset you?”

That was worse. An actual question required an actual answer.

I said, “The girl at assembly.”

He blinked. “My cousin?”

Somewhere, the dictionaries were probably laughing at me.
@16
Her name was Kavya. She had transferred that term, lived with his family during the week, and apparently considered his appearance a public responsibility.

“She was removing a price sticker,” he said. “New shirt.”

“Oh. Cousin-ah? Seri. Naan summa dhaan ketten.”

Nobody in the history of questions had asked less casually.

“Did you think she was doing surgery?”

“Podhum. Poi edhavadhu alphabetise pannu. Please.”

He did not tease me about what I had really meant. That small kindness made my embarrassment bearable.

Later, a note appeared in the folder.

Important update: no stickers remain.

I wrote back: Your medical recovery inspires us all.

The country reopened its borders.
@17
The birthday argument happened over donated calendars.

He mentioned his birthday. I said that was impossible because it was mine.

“You don't own the date.”

“Naan first vandhen. Seniority-ku konjam respect kudu.”

“Exactly one year first.”

We compared the years printed on our school identity cards. Same day, same month, one year apart. I was sixteen; he was fifteen. Simple maths, unusually distracting consequences.

“Senior citizen,” he said.

“Respect your elders.”

“Would you like the chair?”

I threw a blue label at him.

It stuck to his sleeve. He left it there until lunch ended.
@18
Revathi ma'am announced an interschool storytelling event. Our reading project could send a student-written story and a short performance.

Nila volunteered me before I had finished inhaling.

“He can handle sound,” ma'am added, looking at Umbrella Boy. “Only if your classwork is up to date.”

He nodded, but his fingers tightened on the folder.

Afterwards I asked whether he disliked stages.

“Stages are fine. Unexpected microphones, less fine.”

I didn't understand yet.

“All right,” I said. “We'll make nothing unexpected.”

It was my first promise to him. Small enough to keep, I hoped.
@19
I wrote a story about a train that stopped for people who weren't ready to say goodbye. It was dramatic. There was weather. Nila accused me of personally overwatering Indian Railways.

He read quietly, then tapped one line.

“Why does the girl give up her ticket?”

“For love.”

“Could the boy buy another ticket instead?”

I stared at him.

“Avalukku oru dream irukku, illa?” he said. “She wanted to go somewhere. She should get to go.”

I crossed out the ending.

That evening I wrote a better one, and for the first time, the girl boarded the train.
@20
The final script disappeared two days before submission.

My folder was on the workroom table. The inventory remained. The jokes remained. Twelve handwritten story pages were gone.

Mrs. Mary hadn't moved them. Nila hadn't borrowed them. Revathi ma'am asked us to check calmly before accusing anyone.

Then I saw a photograph in the class group: another student's entry, already submitted.

The title was different.

The opening sentence was mine.

Under the photograph, someone had commented that our library boy had helped prepare it.

For the first time, I wished I had never answered his note.
# 3 | The wrong version
@21
At lunch I held out my phone like evidence in a courtroom.

“Did you give him my story?”

He read the screen. His expression changed.

“I typed a script for Dev. I didn't know it was yours.”

“You read mine.”

“Only the first draft. His opened in a classroom.”

The photograph showed the second page, cropped above the borrowed sentence. My anger had arrived before the facts, carrying a loudspeaker.

“Romba convenient-ah irukku,” I said, letting my hurt do all the talking.

He set the phone down carefully.

“Ask me again when you want an answer you haven't already decided.”
@22
I hated him for that sentence until approximately four o'clock, when I hated myself for needing it.

At home, I found photographs I'd taken of my draft to send Nila. The dates were clear. I sent them to Revathi ma'am with a factual explanation.

No accusations about motives. No dramatic status update. Amma sat beside me until I pressed send.

“You can be hurt and still be accurate,” she said.

I wanted her to say everybody else was terrible.

Parents are very unreliable when you need professional assistance with being unreasonable.
@23
Ma'am met us with Dev and Mrs. Mary the next afternoon. Dev admitted taking the loose pages to photocopy an example of structure. He'd returned them to the wrong cupboard, then kept more than structure in his entry.

He apologised without looking at me.

Umbrella Boy had typed only what Dev gave him. His file history showed the earlier opening.

The entry was withdrawn. Ma'am asked Dev to write a new piece and credit any help.

Cinema-la vara madhiri dramatic punishment edhuvum illa. There was no grand scene.

Just an embarrassed boy, a corrected mistake, and my own apology still waiting to be said.
@24
I found him beside the water cooler.

“I should have listened.”

“Yes.”

A terrible response. Accurate responses often are.

“I was scared someone had taken the only thing I was good at.”

He leaned against the wall, considering that.

“Pages-a eduthukkalam, Shreya. Un talent-a edukka mudiyadhu.” He said it simply, as if that was the one fact I shouldn't have doubted.

I looked down at my shoes.

“I'm sorry,” I said, without adding but.

He nodded. “Next time, ask first.”

“Next time, please don't speak like a very young headmaster.”

His mouth twitched.

Our friendship didn't magically repair itself. But it accepted the first careful stitch.
@25
We rebuilt the script over three lunches. I wrote. Nila performed unrequested voices. He recorded sound cues on the school's old laptop.

When the rehearsal microphone squealed, he went still.

Not irritated. Frightened.

I turned the volume down and asked if he wanted a break. He nodded once.

Outside, he watched the courtyard until his breathing settled.

“At my old school, I froze during a speech,” he said. “Someone shared the video.”

I waited.

“People kept playing it.”

For once, I had no joke ready. I was grateful for that.
@26
“Nee stage-ku poganum-nu avasiyam illa,” I said. “Only do the part you actually want.”

“I know. I still want to do sound.”

“Then we'll test it before everyone arrives.”

He looked relieved that I wasn't turning his fear into a motivational speech.

We wrote a cue list with backup options. If the sound failed, Nila would keep reading. If he needed to leave, a teacher would take over.

Practical things. Boring things, probably, in a cinema romance.

But when he returned to the rehearsal room beside me, I felt something warmer than butterflies.

I felt trusted.
@27
On competition morning, the school van refused to start.

Nila declared this sabotage by the universe. Revathi ma'am arranged another van. We arrived with eight minutes to spare and a box of props on my lap.

At the entrance, I tripped over my own shoelace again.

He looked at it, then at me.

“Recurring theme.”

“Idhu en signature entry. Character consistency.”

He held the props while I tied the lace properly.

Inside the hall, a hundred unfamiliar faces turned towards the stage.

His hand tightened on the cue sheet.

I pointed to our backup plan. He nodded and stepped forward.
@28
We did not win.

Please adjust the imaginary background music accordingly.

We came third. Nila skipped an entire paragraph, I dropped a cardboard station sign, and the final train whistle sounded suspiciously like a kettle.

But he managed every cue. When the applause came, he looked surprised by it.

I clapped for him until my palms stung.

On the way out, a judge stopped me. “Your ending lets the girl have a future. Keep that.”

I glanced at the boy carrying our ridiculous station sign.

“I will,” I said.

I meant more than the story.
@29
We shared a packet of biscuits in the van, passing it through Nila because she had appointed herself the international border.

“Ungala vechukittu mudiyala,” she said. “Please, just be normal for one biscuit packet.”

He offered me the last biscuit.

I broke it in half.

Such a tiny thing. No violins. No slow motion. Only crumbs on my skirt and afternoon traffic outside.

Yet I remembered how he'd wanted to save the torn books, how he had asked why my heroine must stay behind.

My heart, traitor that it was, quietly changed his classification.

Not just friend.

Oh, kadavule.
@30
Monday's note was waiting in the blue folder.

Thank you for making a plan instead of making a speech.

Below it sat a pressed yellow flower, flattened between two scraps of clean paper. Not a rose. Probably something he'd rescued from the courtyard.

I carried it home inside my notebook like a highly confidential national document.

That evening his class teacher posted an updated project rota.

His name was absent from every remaining Saturday.

I checked twice.

Then a message arrived from Kavya: If he seems distracted tomorrow, please don't take it personally.
# 4 | An empty chair
@31
He wasn't at school the next day.

Or the next.

The blue folder stayed painfully organised. There were no new umbrellas in the margin, no complaints about my label spacing.

Kavya said there was a family situation, and I didn't push her for details. I asked whether I could send the homework through her.

“Please,” she said.

I copied the assignments carefully, then added a note on separate paper.

Reply panna vendam. First veetla ellam paathukko. Your alphabet is safe with me.

I erased a heart I hadn't meant to draw.

The little dent in the paper remained.
@32
On Friday, he returned with tired eyes and no lunch box.

I offered half of mine. He initially refused, then accepted when I said Amma had packed enough lemon rice to feed a cricket team.

“My mother had surgery,” he said quietly. “She's recovering. I'm helping at home.”

“Aunty recover aaguradhu romba santhosham. Nee saaptiya?” I asked, moving the lunch box between us.

He nodded, looking at the food.

I didn't say everything happened for a reason. I couldn't imagine a reason good enough to make his week necessary.

Instead, I passed the potato fry.

For that lunch, it was what I could give.
@33
The Saturdays he'd missed had become weekday chores: collecting groceries, helping his younger sister with homework, answering relatives who all wanted updates at once.

“Her name is Anu,” he said of his sister. “She's nine and thinks I'm a badly paid assistant.”

“You're paid?”

“In criticism.”

That sounded like a sibling.

He smiled properly for the first time that week.

I wanted to fix every difficult thing. Instead, I asked what project work he could reasonably manage.

“Don't give away my chair,” he said.

“I was considering renting it.”

“Terrible business plan. It wobbles.”
@34
Nila saw us sharing lunch and asked later whether we were together.

“No.”

“Do you want to be?”

I stared at the timetable as if it contained spiritual guidance.

“I don't know how to be somebody's girlfriend.”

“Adhukku certificate edhuvum thevai illa, madam. Most people learn as they go.”

“He has enough going on.”

“Then be his friend. That's not a waiting room.”

I looked at her.

Sometimes Nila said something so sensible that I worried she'd been replaced overnight.

We walked to class together. I put my arm through hers, and she let the subject rest.
@35
My first letter wasn't delivered.

It began, I like the way you remember small things, which sounded dangerously close to a complaint about surveillance.

The second version began, This is embarrassing, which was honest but lacked ambition.

The third simply said, I think about you when you're not here.

I folded it and hid it inside my story notebook.

Then Amma called me for dinner, and I spent twenty minutes discussing school fees while my entire emotional future sat beside an unfinished chemistry equation.

Being sixteen involved a truly unreasonable amount of multitasking.
@36
At the reading centre, Thara asked where her usual storyteller was.

“Helping his family,” I said.

“Then you do the dragon voice.”

My dragon sounded like an uncle with a sore throat. She accepted the effort and corrected the ending.

“Sorry sonna mattum podhuma?” Thara demanded. “The dragon can't keep doing it again.”

Mrs. Mary looked up from her book repairs. “An excellent editorial note.”

I wrote Thara's sentence in the project folder.

That evening I thought about apologies, including my own.

I decided the next time something frightened me, I would ask a question before inventing an answer.
@37
He read the folder on Monday and laughed at my description of the dragon's vocal difficulties.

Then he turned serious.

“Can I tell you something without you feeling you must fix it?”

I nodded.

His family was considering a move to his grandmother's town for a year. It would make his mother's recovery and the household expenses easier. Nothing was final.

The room seemed to tilt very slightly.

“Do you want to go?”

“I want everyone to be okay.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

“I know,” he said. “It's the answer I have.”
@38
I cried on the bus home, quietly enough that the conductor asked whether I'd lost money.

“Something like that,” I said.

Nothing had happened. He hadn't left. We weren't together. I had no official reason to feel abandoned.

En manasukku indha logic ellam puriyala. My heart apparently did not require paperwork.

At home, I washed my face and helped Amma cut beans. She didn't interrogate me.

After a while she said, “You can tell me only the part you understand.”

So I told her about a friend who might move away.

She handed me a smaller knife and listened.
@39
Amma didn't promise that things would work out.

“You can care about someone without knowing where the story goes,” she said.

“Idhu romba kashtam, Amma. That sounds stressful.”

“It is.”

Very inspiring household. Five stars.

She nudged the bowl closer. “But don't punish them for a choice they may not have.”

The next morning I returned to the workroom with two sharpened pencils and my normal terrible handwriting.

We worked on the Saturday programme.

I didn't ask him to promise he would stay.

When our elbows touched over the book list, neither of us moved immediately.
@40
Revathi ma'am asked me to remain after the project meeting.

“I've recommended your writing for a residential summer workshop in Chennai,” she said. “There may be a fee waiver.”

For a moment, excitement beat everything else.

Then I looked through the window at the boy waiting near the courtyard.

A summer away. A possible move. Two directions opening at once.

Ma'am followed my glance but said only, “Take the form home. This is your decision with your family.”

I put it in the blue notebook.

Beside the letter I still hadn't delivered.
# 5 | The paper boat
@41
“You should apply,” he said immediately.

I had shown him the workshop form hoping, embarrassingly, that he would look a little sad first.

“That's it?”

“What else?”

“Nothing.”

He studied me for a moment.

“Unna miss pannuven. Aana nee happy-ah irundha, enakkum santhosham dhaan. Those aren't opposite things.”

I became intensely interested in a glue stain.

“Who said you'd miss me?”

“I just did, Shreya.”

The way he said my name was unfair. Mine was available to him any time he wanted, while I could barely get an ordinary sentence past my own heartbeat.
@42
The application required a new story. I wrote about a girl who collected other people's unfinished goodbyes in a biscuit tin.

He didn't edit it for me. He read it and asked three questions, including why the girl never kept anything for herself.

I changed a scene. Nila corrected an unfortunate sentence that accidentally made the biscuit tin fall in love with a postman.

Amma signed the form.

Appa calculated the travel cost twice and said we would manage if the fee waiver came.

For once, my future felt like something several people were holding carefully.
@43
Our shared birthday fell on a school day.

I brought chocolates for my class and one extra packet of orange sweets, because he always traded for those.

He brought a bookmark he'd cut from sturdy card. On one side was a tiny umbrella. On the other: For stories with better endings.

“Senior discount?” I asked.

“Nee books-a konjam careful-ah handle panna, discount consider pannalam.”

I was seventeen now. He was sixteen. Still exactly one year apart, still equally incapable of saying the obvious.

Nila watched us exchange gifts and announced that she deserved cake for her suffering.
@44
The afternoon rain trapped us under the school verandah with half the building. Teachers called parents. Shoes squeaked. Somebody's tiffin leaked sambar into a bag.

He folded a scrap of unused paper into a boat.

“Super plan. Idhula rendu perum escape aagalam.”

“For your dramatic thoughts,” he said. “They need transport.”

I took it. Inside, beneath the crease, was a sentence.

I like the days you're in them.

I looked up.

He wasn't smiling now.

The rain made enough noise for every student on the verandah, but I heard only my own small inhale.
@45
“Project days?” I asked, because apparently I wanted romance delivered with an itemised invoice.

“Ella naalum, Shreya. Nee irukkura naal konjam nalla irukku.”

“Oh.”

He looked suddenly worried. “You don't have to answer now.”

I unfolded the boat carefully, smoothing the paper against my notebook.

“I like those days too.”

It was not an eloquent declaration. Nobody could frame it over a fireplace.

But his shoulders relaxed, and my face grew hot, and the world continued around us as if something enormous hadn't just become true.

Nila arrived holding three umbrellas.

“Why do you both look guilty of literature?” she asked.
@46
We didn't announce anything. We had a conversation, which turned out to be much harder than a dramatic confession.

“I want us to keep our friends,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Padippayum paakanum. Love vandhadhum chemistry-ku goodbye solla mudiyadhu.”

He laughed. “No messages when someone needs to work. No guessing games if we're upset.”

I looked meaningfully at the paper boat.

“That was one guessing game,” he admitted.

We agreed there was no rush to name everything.

Then the bell rang, and we went to different classrooms, both smiling like we had misread the timetable in a wonderful way.
@47
For one week, life became suspiciously kind.

He saved the seat closest to the fan. I remembered he hated coriander stems. Nila stopped teasing for almost forty minutes, a personal record.

At the centre, Thara asked why my dragon sounded happy.

“Improved working conditions,” I said.

He had to put down the book to laugh.

On Friday, we finished restoring our hundredth donated book. Mrs. Mary took a group photograph, making everybody hold a book instead of posing with empty hands.

I stood beside him.

Our little fingers brushed, and mine stayed there for a second.
@48
The workshop acceptance arrived on a Sunday.

Full fee waiver. Three weeks. My name typed correctly, which somehow made it more real.

I called Nila first because she would scream without asking sensible travel questions. Then I told my parents. Then I messaged him.

His reply came immediately: I knew your girl with the biscuit tin would make it.

A minute later: Proud of you.

I read those three words until the screen dimmed.

Then another message appeared.

Can we talk tomorrow? The move has been decided.

Happiness and dread sat down beside each other in my room.
@49
He would leave at the end of term. His mother was recovering steadily, but the family's plan made sense. His grandmother had space. His father's work could move. School admission was arranged.

I listened to all the sensible reasons and disliked every one.

“When?”

“The Saturday you leave for the workshop.”

Of course. Vera naale kedaikkalaya, universe? It enjoyed symmetry when it was being annoying.

“We can stay in touch,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Properly.”

“Yes.”

Neither of us knew yet how difficult properly could become when distance arrived with responsibilities, shared phones, and words that reached the wrong place.
@50
That night I took out the letter I'd hidden weeks earlier.

I think about you when you're not here.

It seemed too small now. I added a page about his patient silences, the repaired books, the way he had wanted my heroine to catch her train.

At the end I wrote: I like you. Not just the idea of you. The actual irritating person.

I signed it Shreya and drew one terrible umbrella.

The envelope was cream, borrowed from Appa's desk.

On the front, I wrote a private instruction: Open when you miss our wobbly chair.
